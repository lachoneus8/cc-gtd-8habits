# Habit 2 — Begin With the End in Mind

One file for this habit: the research behind it, and the shared content
`/onboard`, `/habit`, and `/weekly`'s Habit Calibration read directly — never copied into
`personal-untracked/`. Its GTD hooks are `personal-untracked/identity.md` (mission/roles) and each
project's `info.md` "Desired Outcome" field (see the GTD Integration Map in `[[overall_plan]]`).
See `habits/README.md` for the shape every habit's file follows.

## Research

Raw material for [[task_list]] C.2's design step, not the habit's shipped content itself. Rooted
in Covey's own text throughout — the rest is what practitioners, coaches, and adjacent fields
have found in applying it since.

### Core Concept, Briefly

- **Two creations.** All things are created twice: a mental (first) creation, then a physical
  (second) creation. The mental creation is the blueprint; if you don't consciously write it,
  someone or something else — other people's agendas, habit, circumstance — will. This is the
  principle the habit rests on, and the one that gives Habit 1's choice something to aim at.
- **Begin with the end in mind.** Know where you're going, so today's steps are always in the
  right direction. Covey's warning image: a ladder leaned against the wrong wall — efficient
  climbing that lands somewhere you didn't want to be.
- **Personal mission statement.** The written form of the first creation: character (who you
  want to be), contribution (what you want to do), and the values and principles underneath both.
  Covey says it should take real time and reflection, and be revised as you understand more.
- **Life center.** Covey lists common centers people unknowingly live from — spouse, family,
  money, work, possessions, pleasure, friends/enemies, church, self — each with predictable
  weaknesses in security, guidance, wisdom, and power. His recommended center is *principles*,
  the only one stable enough to steer by. Worth surfacing, because most people can't answer
  "what's my center?" until they see the list.
- **Roles and goals.** The mission gets made concrete by naming your major roles and
  long-term goals per role. (Covey's *weekly* use of roles — choosing a "big rock" per role each
  week — belongs to Habit 3; noted here only so C.2/C.3 don't build it early.)
- **Leadership before management.** Habit 2 is leadership (are we climbing the right wall?);
  Habit 3 is management (climbing efficiently). Habit 2 work that gets reduced to a to-do list
  has been misfiled.

### Practical Exercises Practitioners Use

- **Funeral visualization (Covey's own).** Imagine attending your own funeral and hearing four
  speakers — family, friend, work colleague, community/church contact. What would you want each to
  say about the person you were? The point is to surface values, not to produce a eulogy.
- **Variants on the time horizon.** Practitioners offer gentler or more concrete alternatives for
  people the funeral frame doesn't suit: a one-year-to-live prompt, a 25th-anniversary or
  retirement-party speech, or "what do you want to be true in 10/20 years" horizons. Same
  function — a vantage point outside the present urgency.
- **Roles inventory.** List every role you hold (self, partner, parent, friend, professional,
  community, etc.), then for each write what "doing it well" would look like in the long run and
  one or two long-term goals. Practitioners commonly find one role (often "self") missing or
  neglected on the first pass.
- **Mission-statement drafting formulas.** Covey's three elements (character / contribution /
  values). A widely used practitioner template is "To do [action] for [group] to [impact]" —
  useful as a first scaffold, more restrictive than Covey's own framing. Psychology-oriented
  coaches also pair it with 1/5/10/20-year goal horizons to check alignment.
- **Values identification.** Pick core values from a list and rank or narrow them, then test
  each against real recent decisions (did I act on this when it cost something?). Practitioners
  stress that *lived* values, not aspirational ones, are the starting evidence.
- **Draft, live with it, revise.** Multiple sources agree a mission statement is versioned, not
  final: write a rough draft, carry it for weeks, and rewrite where it rings false. Covey himself
  describes it as a years-long process, revised as circumstances and understanding change.
- **Review before planning.** Re-reading the mission before weekly planning is the practitioner
  habit most consistently credited with keeping it alive — a statement that's written once and
  filed does nothing. (This is the hook for the weekly review, not a Habit 3 mechanic.)
- **Visualization and affirmation (Covey's "Habit 2 tools").** Vivid mental rehearsal of acting
  on a value in a situation you expect to find hard, and affirmations written to be personal,
  positive, present-tense, visual, and emotional. Evaluate before adopting: the visualization
  half is well supported in practice; the affirmation half is the part most likely to feel
  hollow to a skeptical user.

### Cross-Disciplinary Grounding

- **Purpose in life as a well-being dimension.** Ryff's psychological well-being model treats
  purpose (goals, direction, sense that life has meaning) as one of six core dimensions.
  Research links stronger sense of purpose/meaning with higher well-being and life satisfaction
  and, in longitudinal work, lower mortality risk. Independent support for the habit's premise.
- **Self-concordant goals** (Sheldon & Elliot, 1999). Goals that express a person's own values
  are pursued more persistently and produce more well-being when attained than goals adopted
  from pressure or guilt. Direct empirical parallel to why the mission has to be user-authored,
  not handed over — and a warning against copying an admired mission statement.
- **ACT: values vs. goals.** Acceptance and Commitment Therapy separates *values* (directions
  you keep moving in, never "completed") from *goals* (achievable outcomes along the way). This
  maps cleanly onto Covey's mission (direction) vs. long-term goals (destinations), and gives
  vocabulary for a common confusion: writing a goal into the mission statement and then feeling
  the mission is "done" or "failed."
- **Frankl and meaning.** Habit 2's emphasis on contribution echoes Frankl's view that meaning is
  found through what one gives and commits to, not only what one feels. (Same caution as Habit 1:
  cite Frankl for his own writing, not for the misattributed stimulus/response line.)
- **Ikigai** (passion, mission, vocation, profession) is a popular adjacent frame for the same
  question. Useful as an alternate on-ramp for someone who finds Covey's wording abstract, but
  note it's oriented toward livelihood fit, where Covey's is oriented toward principles and
  character — don't let it displace the latter.
- **GTD's horizons of focus** (David Allen). GTD's higher altitudes — purpose/principles,
  vision, goals, areas of focus — are the same ground Habit 2 covers, reviewed on a longer
  cadence than the weekly review. The overlap is the strongest structural argument for reusing
  `identity.md` and each project's Desired Outcome rather than adding new machinery. Allen's
  natural planning model (purpose → vision of outcome → brainstorm → organize → next action) is
  also literally "begin with the end in mind" at project scale.

### Common Pitfalls / Misapplications

- **Platitude statements.** The most common critique: many mission statements collapse into
  "be a good person" clichés that couldn't guide any actual decision. Test to apply in coaching:
  could this sentence have settled a real choice in the last month? If not, it's too generic.
- **Writing it once and filing it.** A statement that isn't revisited or wired into planning has
  no effect. This is the failure mode this repo's design most has to guard against, since the
  user's prior GTD system lapsed on the regular-discipline layer, not the writing layer.
- **Perfectionism and false starts.** Treating the mission as a finished, permanent deliverable
  stalls people at the blank page. Framing it as a first draft that gets revised is what several
  practitioners say gets people writing at all.
- **Aspiration instead of evidence.** Writing values you admire rather than values your recent
  choices show. Better sources start from what you actually did and what you noticed you
  cared about, then ask whether it's what you want.
- **Confusing mission with goals or role duties.** Goals go stale when achieved, and role
  lists describe obligations; the mission is the direction under both. See ACT above.
- **Borrowed or externally imposed content.** Copying an example, a template's sample text, or
  another person's values. Self-concordance research and Covey's own emphasis on inside-out
  both say this backfires. It also means an LLM assistant must not invent or polish a user's
  mission for them — consistent with this repo's rule that values content stays user-authored.
- **Funeral visualization can land badly.** For grief, anxiety, or health circumstances, the
  exercise can be distressing. Offer the alternate horizons above and let the user choose.
- **Center-blindness.** People often can't see their real life center until they read the list;
  skipping that step leaves the mission floating on top of whatever they actually organize
  their life around.

### Hooks for This System

- **`identity.md` is the artifact.** Already holds starter anchors, a rough Roles list, and a
  one-sentence Mission draft from Foundation (A.5/A.6). Habit 2's work is deepening those in the
  user's own words, not creating a second file.
- **Project `info.md` "Desired Outcome"** is Habit 2 at project scale (Allen's outcome
  vision). **Wired (C.3):** `/capture` asks for the outcome and an optional role/value link when
  a new project is opened, and `/weekly`'s project review raises empty or role-less outcomes as
  `EDIT` blocks. The project templates are deliberately unchanged, so GTD works standalone.
- **`/weekly`'s Identity Calibration** already compares present reality with `identity.md`;
  Habit 2's Calibration Criteria complement it. **Line drawn (C.3):** Identity Calibration =
  did behavior match the anchors; Habit 2 calibration = is the direction being used. `/weekly`
  says to reference a finding rather than restate it in both.
- **Review-before-planning — adopted for `/weekly`, not for `/daily` (C.3).** `/weekly` shows
  Roles and Mission read-only before the project review. `/daily` was evaluated and **not
  adopted**: its Top 3/time-blocking is Habit 3's ground, and a daily mission recital adds
  friction to the command people run most. The weekly read-through is the cadence Covey's own
  weekly planning uses.
- **Nothing here writes `identity.md`.** Every C.3 hook is read-only or asks the user; edits to
  Roles/Mission happen only on explicit instruction, in their words.
- **Not for this phase:** weekly per-role "big rocks" and Quadrant II scheduling (Habit 3, D.x).
- **Constraint for C.4:** any LLM assistance may question, organize, reflect back, and
  pressure-test wording, but never authors mission content unprompted.

## Habit Content

The shipped content — read directly by `/onboard`, `/habit`, and `/weekly`, never copied into
`personal-untracked/`. Designed for [[task_list]] C.2 from the Research above. Everything the
user produces here lands in their own `identity.md` (Roles, Mission) or a project's `info.md`
Desired Outcome, in their own words — this file only supplies the questions and the structure.

### Principle

Everything gets made twice: first in your head, then in the world. If you don't decide on
purpose what you're building toward, the first draft gets written for you — by other people's
expectations, by habit, by whatever's loudest this week. Beginning with the end in mind means
deciding who you want to be and what you want to contribute, then letting that steer today's
choices.

Habit 1 gave you the freedom to choose your response; this gives that choice a direction.
Without it you can climb very efficiently up the wrong wall. The direction lives in your
`identity.md` — your roles and a mission you write yourself, in your own words, and revise as you
learn more. It's a compass, not a finish line: it's never "done," and a rough draft you actually
use beats a polished one you don't.

### Reflection Prompts

- Picture the people who know you best describing you at a milestone years from now. What do
  you hope each of them would say — and how close is your current week to earning that?
- Which of your roles gets the most of your attention right now, and which gets the least? Is
  that split a choice, or just what happened?
- Think of something you worked hard on recently. Whose goal was it — yours, or someone else's
  that you picked up? Would you choose it again knowing what it cost?
- Look at your current Mission draft. Could it have settled a real decision you made this month?
  If not, what would make it sharper?
- Where do you notice yourself organizing your days around something other than what you say
  you value — work, approval, money, a relationship, comfort?

### Practice

- **Ladder check.** Before committing to a new project or significant commitment, ask one
  question: what is this for, and does it lead somewhere I actually want to go? Then write it as
  the project's Desired Outcome, in your words. Small, and it's the habit at its working size.
- **Read the mission.** Once a week, before planning, re-read your Roles and Mission in
  `identity.md` and note anything that rings false or has changed. Editing it is part of the
  practice — revising is the point, not a sign it failed.

**Going deeper (optional).** For a `/habit` session with more time, in roughly this order — take
one per session, don't try to do them all at once:

1. **Perspective exercise.** Covey's funeral visualization (four speakers: family, friend, work,
   community) — or, if that frame doesn't suit you, a gentler one: a milestone-anniversary speech,
   or "what's true in 10 / 20 years." Goal is values that surface, not a eulogy. Always offer the
   alternate, and stop if it's distressing.
2. **Roles inventory.** List every role you hold (including "self"), what doing each well would
   look like long-term, and one or two long-term goals per role. Check whether a role is
   missing or starved.
3. **Values from evidence.** Start from what your recent choices show you cared about, not from
   a list of values you admire; then ask whether that's what you want to be true.
4. **Center check.** Look at Covey's list of common life centers (family, work, money,
   possessions, pleasure, friends, church, self, principles) and ask which one your days actually
   run on. Name it honestly before drafting.
5. **Mission draft or revision.** Write it yourself — character, contribution, and the values
   underneath — as a first draft to carry for a few weeks, not a final statement. Assistance is
   limited to questions, reflecting your own words back, and pressure-testing (see Guardrails).
6. **Project outcome sweep.** Walk active projects and check each Desired Outcome traces to a
   role or value; surface any that don't as candidates to keep, reshape, or drop — the user
   decides.

### Guardrails for the LLM

- **The user authors it.** Never draft, polish, or "improve" Roles or Mission text unprompted,
  and never offer sample mission language they could adopt wholesale. Ask, reflect back, and
  point out vague or contradictory wording; write to `identity.md` only on their explicit
  instruction, using their words.
- **Test for platitudes.** If a statement is generic enough to fit anyone, say so and ask what
  a real decision in their life would look like under it.
- **Values are directions, goals are destinations.** If a goal is written into the Mission, or
  the Mission phrased as something that can be finished, name it and let them choose.
- **Evidence over aspiration.** When values are being named, ask for a recent concrete choice
  that shows it.
- **Don't build Habit 3 early.** Weekly per-role commitments and prioritization belong to Habit 3;
  here, roles are for direction and reflection.

### Calibration Criteria

What `/weekly`'s Habit Calibration looks for as evidence, from the user's own journal/daily
entries — per the Weekly Calibration Contract in `habits/README.md`. This complements Identity
Calibration rather than repeating it: Identity Calibration asks whether the week's behavior
matched the anchors in `identity.md`; these ask whether the user is actually *using* the
direction — deciding by it, revisiting it, and starting from outcomes.

- **Decisions by direction.** Their own words showing a choice made by reference to a value,
  role, or intended outcome ("I said no because…"), vs. choices made on urgency, mood, or
  other people's asks alone.
- **Outcome-first framing.** New commitments and projects that arrive with a stated desired
  outcome or purpose, vs. taken on as bare tasks with no "what for."
- **Mission in use.** Evidence the Roles/Mission were re-read, questioned, or revised this
  week (including deliberately leaving them unchanged), vs. untouched and unreferenced.
- **Role attention.** Whether time and energy reached the roles they say matter, and whether a
  neglected role was noticed and named rather than drifted past.
- **Honesty about the gap.** Willingness to write down where the week didn't match what they
  say they want, vs. only recording what looks aligned.

Because the Mission is meant to stay rough at first, an empty or placeholder Mission is a
🟡 with a gentle nudge toward a `/habit` session — not a 🔴 and not a reason to nag.
