## Research

Notes gathered for [[task_list]] B.1, rooted in Covey's own text throughout — the rest is what
practitioners, coaches, and adjacent fields have found in applying it since.

### Core Concept, Briefly

- **Circle of Concern vs. Circle of Influence.** The Circle of Concern holds everything you
  worry about; the Circle of Influence holds what you can actually affect. Proactive people
  spend their energy inside the Influence circle; reactive people spend it in the (larger)
  Concern circle and feel like victims of it.
- **Response-ability.** Between a stimulus and your response is a space — the freedom to choose.
  Covey frames this as the foundation the other six habits build on.
- **Proactive vs. reactive language.** Language both reveals and reinforces which circle someone
  is operating from — "I can't" / "I have to" / "he makes me so mad" vs. "I choose" / "I can
  choose a different approach" / "let's look at our alternatives."

### Practical Exercises Practitioners Use

- **Circle-mapping.** Draw three concentric circles (Concern / Influence / Control). Write
  current worries on notes, sort them into the correct circle, then pick one item in Influence
  or Control and name a next action for it. Coaches recommend redoing this periodically, not
  once — the sort shifts as you act.
- **Language-monitoring.** Spend a set period (a day, a week) just noticing your own reactive
  phrases without judgment, then start swapping in proactive alternatives. Several practitioner
  writeups frame this as the single highest-leverage, lowest-effort entry point into the habit.
- **Small-action momentum.** Rather than tackling a whole Concern-circle worry, find the
  smallest available action inside your actual Influence and take it. Multiple sources describe
  this as a "virtuous cycle" — small proactive wins measurably grow what's in your Influence
  circle over time, which is Covey's own claim about the circles being dynamic, not fixed.
- **The "circle check" pause.** When you notice you're ruminating on something in your Concern
  circle, stop, do something small and concrete in your Control circle (a short walk, a glass of
  water), then re-approach the situation from Influence. Useful as an in-the-moment technique,
  distinct from the periodic full circle-mapping exercise above.
- **30-day proactivity journal.** Covey himself proposes a 30-day proactive-living challenge.
  Practitioners have since operationalized it as a short daily journal prompt: *Did I act on my
  commitment to be proactive today? Was today's frustration actually in my Circle of Influence?
  Where did I catch myself using reactive language?*

### Cross-Disciplinary Grounding

- **Stoicism's dichotomy of control** (Epictetus) is the same core distinction — control what's
  yours, accept what isn't — and long predates Covey. Covey's addition, and the one worth
  keeping distinct in this system's framing, is that the Influence circle isn't static: using it
  well grows it, where Stoicism is more focused on acceptance than on this expansion loop.
- **Locus-of-control research** (organizational psychology) gives Habit 1 empirical backing
  outside the self-help genre: an internal locus of control correlates with resilience,
  proactive coping, better job performance, and buffering against workplace stress — a
  research-grounded parallel to "operate from your Circle of Influence."
- **The "stimulus-response space" quote**, almost always attributed to Viktor Frankl, doesn't
  actually appear in his writing — Covey himself said he found it unattributed and used it to
  characterize Frankl's ideas. Worth knowing so this system doesn't over-cite it as a direct
  Frankl quote if it ever gets surfaced to the user.

### Common Pitfalls / Misapplications

- **Chronic Concern-circle focus is self-reinforcing in the wrong direction.** Sources tie this
  to stress physiology, not just mindset: dwelling on what you can't control narrows the very
  motivation and decision-making capacity you'd need to act proactively, making someone *more*
  reactive over time. Worth naming in coaching/reflection prompts as a warning sign, not just a
  neutral state.
- **Proactivity isn't toxic positivity, and it isn't "your circumstances are your fault."**
  Covey's own framing is about choosing your *response* within real constraints, not about
  denying that constraints exist. Practitioner critique of how this habit gets misapplied
  clusters here: treating "focus on your influence" as a reason to dismiss someone's real
  systemic or circumstantial limits, or to suppress legitimate difficult emotions in the name of
  staying positive. The Habit Content below stays on the "choose your response" side of that
  line, not either extreme.

## Habit Content

### Principle

Between what happens to you and what you do next, there's a gap. That gap is yours — nobody
else's. Being proactive means choosing your response there, on purpose, based on what you
actually value, instead of reacting on mood or circumstance.

Not everything is yours to act on. Sort what's actually in front of you into what you can do
something about (your **Circle of Influence**) and what you can only watch or worry about (your
**Circle of Concern**). Spend your energy in the Concern circle and you end up feeling like
things just happen to you. Spend it in the Influence circle instead, and — used consistently —
that circle tends to grow.

### Reflection Prompts

- Where this week did you catch yourself thinking or saying "I have to" or "there's nothing I
  can do"? What would the proactive version of that sentence have been?
- Name something you've spent real energy worrying about lately. Is any piece of it actually
  inside your influence — or is all of it Concern-circle?
- Think of a recent mistake or setback. Did you catch it and adjust quickly, or did it sit until
  it got bigger?
- What's one thing inside your Circle of Influence you've been putting off?

### Practice

- **Language watch.** For a few days, just notice when you use reactive language ("I can't," "I
  have to," "they made me") — without forcing an in-the-moment rewrite. Bring the pattern to
  your next `/habit` session or journal entry.
- **Circle check.** When you catch yourself stuck ruminating on something you can't control,
  pause, do one small concrete thing you *can* control, then look again at what's actually in
  your influence.

**Going deeper (optional).** For a `/habit` session with more time: a full circle-mapping
exercise (list current worries, sort each into Concern / Influence / Control, pick one
actionable item), or Covey's own 30-day proactivity challenge — a daily journal line on whether
today's frustration was actually in your influence, and where reactive language crept in. Offer
these, don't default to them.

### Calibration Criteria

What `/weekly`'s Habit Calibration looks for as evidence, from the user's own journal/daily
entries — per the Weekly Calibration Contract in `habits/README.md`:

- **Language.** Proactive phrasing ("I choose," "I can") showing up in their own words, vs.
  reactive phrasing ("I have to," "there's nothing I can do") dominating.
- **Response to setbacks.** Mistakes or problems acknowledged and acted on within the week, vs.
  left to sit or attributed to other people/circumstances.
- **Energy allocation.** Where journal entries spend emotional energy — on something with a
  nameable next action (Influence), vs. open-ended worry with no action attached (Concern).
- **Initiative.** Evidence of acting before being forced to, vs. only responding once something
  became unavoidable.
